Retrieval Fundamentals for Reliable AI Systems

Retrieval is the process of finding relevant information before producing an answer. In a document question-answering system, retrieval turns a broad collection of files into a small evidence set that can fit inside a language model prompt. The system first converts each document segment into a numerical vector, commonly called an embedding. An embedding represents semantic meaning: passages about similar ideas tend to be located near one another in vector space even when they use different vocabulary.

The quality of retrieval often determines the quality of a retrieval-augmented generation system. If the right passage is not retrieved, even a capable language model has no reliable basis for a correct answer. A good retrieval design therefore begins with the source material. Documents should be readable, sufficiently detailed, and accompanied by metadata such as their filename, page number, section title, date, or author. Metadata makes the final answer auditable and helps a reader return to the original material.

Chunking is a necessary bridge between long documents and retrieval. A full report may contain many ideas, but an embedding represents it as one vector. Splitting the report into smaller chunks allows a query about one topic to retrieve only the relevant portion. Chunks that are too large mix unrelated concepts and consume too much prompt space. Chunks that are too small can omit the explanation needed to understand a fact. Sentence-aware chunks of roughly 150 to 250 words are a practical starting point because they preserve grammatical boundaries while remaining focused.

Overlap protects meaning at a boundary. Suppose a definition appears at the end of one chunk and its qualification begins in the next. With no overlap, a search may retrieve only half the explanation. Repeating a modest number of trailing words or sentences in the next chunk reduces this risk. Overlap is not free: it increases index size and can cause near-duplicate retrieval results. A system designer should select an overlap that preserves local context without overwhelming the top results with duplicates.

Similarity search compares the query vector against the stored chunk vectors. Cosine similarity is a common choice because it measures the angle between normalized vectors rather than their length. In practice, a vector index such as FAISS can perform this comparison efficiently. A top-k parameter controls how many passages are returned. A low k can miss complementary evidence; a high k can include distracting context. Exposing k as a command-line option makes the tradeoff visible and easy to evaluate.

Retrieval should not be confused with generation. Retrieval returns evidence and scores. Generation reads evidence and creates a helpful response. The answer generator should be instructed to use only the retrieved passages, cite them precisely, and decline when the passages do not answer the question. This is called grounding. Grounding does not guarantee perfect truth, but it makes the assistant's reasoning inspectable: a user can compare a claim with the passage that supports it.

Evaluation should test both stages. For retrieval, ask whether a relevant chunk appears in the top results. For generation, ask whether the answer is supported, complete, clearly cited, and appropriately uncertain. Include questions that span multiple documents, questions requiring a comparison, and questions that cannot be answered by the collection. An honest "I don't know based on the provided documents" is a successful result for an unsupported question. It is evidence that the system has resisted the temptation to invent an answer.
